# Writing the story

A pretty film with no story is a screensaver. A simple drawing with a real story keeps people
watching to the end. Get this right before you draw anything.

## The shape (every film, any length)
1. **Setup (first 15–20%)**: one main character in one world, and what is normal there. Make the first
   line a hook: show the world in one striking sentence ("In a city made of steel and smoke, nothing
   green had grown for a hundred years.").
2. **The problem (20–45%)**: something goes wrong or something new appears. Make it physical and visible:
   a storm, a wind, a light going out, a lost thing.
3. **The turn (45–70%)**: the character makes one brave or kind choice. This is the heart of the film.
   Name the exact line where it happens.
4. **The payoff (70–92%)**: the choice changes the world, visibly (the city turns green, the lane lights up).
5. **The last line (final 8%)**: a gentle closing sentence, then about 2 seconds to breathe.

## The token
Pick ONE small object that is there at the start and comes back changed at the end: a tiny flame,
a broom, a red flower, a key. Never explain it. The audience finds it, and that is the payoff.
- Tiny Diya: Deepu's small flame. Unnoticed at the start, the source of every flame at the end.
- Little Robot: the broom (cleaning tool → umbrella in the storm → planting tool) and the red flower.

## Test it in three sentences
Write the spine before the narration. If you can't, the story isn't ready.
> A tiny cleaning robot finds the last flower in a grey city. He shields it through a storm until
> his battery almost dies. He sweeps its seeds into every crack, and the city turns green.

## Narration rules
- About **2.2 words per second** at a gentle storybook pace (30 s ≈ 60–70 words, 60 s ≈ 120–130,
  90 s ≈ 180–200).
- 10–13 short lines for a 60 s film. **One picture per line.** If a line can't be drawn, rewrite it.
- Concrete nouns and actions, not feelings told ("his flame held on", not "he felt brave").
- Use a pause "..." before a turn or reveal. It gives you room for a beat on screen.
- Name the character once, early. Simple names that any voice pronounces well (Pip, Boo, Mia).
- No sound words in brackets. Write "Then... whoosh! A cold wind rushed down the lane." in plain text.
- Kids' content: no fear without comfort, no violence, a kind ending.

## The beat list
For every narration line, write what we SEE and how the camera moves:

| # | Line | Picture | Camera |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In a city made of steel and smoke… | Wide grey city, smoke, tiny Pip far below | Slow drift right |
| 2 | Every night, a tiny cleaning robot called Pip… | Night, Pip sweeping under a street lamp | Push in to Pip |
| … | | | |

## Story ideas that work
- A small, overlooked character saves the day (the tiniest lamp, the slowest snail, the quiet kid).
- Kindness to something fragile (a flower, a baby bird, a lost kitten) changes a whole place.
- A fear that turns out to be a friend (the dark, a storm, a shadow that is just an owl).
- Festivals and seasons: Diwali, Christmas, Eid, Halloween, the first snow, spring.
